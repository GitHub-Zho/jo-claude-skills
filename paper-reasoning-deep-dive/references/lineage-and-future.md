# Research lineage and future work

Use this reference to connect the paper to earlier gaps and later responses without inventing a citation story.

## Table of contents

1. Lineage graph
2. Ancestor selection
3. Follow-up classification
4. Verification rules
5. Deriving future work

## 1. Lineage graph

Represent lineage through transferred problems and mechanisms:

```text
Ancestor contribution
→ Ancestor residual limitation
→ Current paper's insight and intervention
→ Limitation resolved, reduced, or untouched
→ New limitation introduced
→ Later paper's response
→ Remaining open question
```

Avoid a flat related-work list. Explain why each cited paper belongs in the chain.

## 2. Ancestor selection

Classify earlier work as:

- **Foundational ancestor**: supplies the core concept, objective, or architecture.
- **Immediate predecessor**: presents the specific method or limitation directly extended.
- **Enabling work**: supplies data, benchmark, tooling, or theory needed by the current paper.
- **Parallel precursor**: pursues the same goal through a materially different route.

Use the current paper's citations as leads, not proof of priority or importance. Inspect the original cited work before attributing a claim to it when access is available.

## 3. Follow-up classification

Classify later work as:

- **Direct follow-up**: explicitly builds on this paper's method or result.
- **Same-team continuation**: continues the line but may change goals or framing.
- **Independent extension**: adopts the idea in a new setting or scale.
- **Corrective work**: challenges, qualifies, or fails to reproduce a claim.
- **Parallel solution**: addresses the same residual gap using another mechanism.

Do not call a paper a follow-up based only on topic similarity.

## 4. Verification rules

Treat publication status, chronology, citation relationships, and “latest” claims as time-sensitive.

When browsing is available:

1. Prefer the paper, publisher, arXiv, author page, official repository, and conference proceedings.
2. Verify title, authors, date, venue or preprint status, and the actual claimed relationship.
3. Read the relevant passage in the later paper; do not rely on search snippets.
4. Distinguish peer-reviewed publication, accepted manuscript, and preprint.
5. Cite sources close to each lineage claim.

When browsing is unavailable, limit lineage to the supplied paper and clearly label unverified memory-derived suggestions.

## 5. Deriving future work

Generate future directions from explicit residual gaps:

```text
Observed or inferred limitation
→ mechanism causing it
→ intervention point
→ proposed change
→ predicted benefit
→ likely new tradeoff
→ decisive evaluation
```

Separate:

- future work proposed by the authors;
- directions already pursued by verified later papers;
- new hypotheses inferred during analysis.

Never present an inferred direction as an existing paper. Prefer a few testable directions over a long speculative list.
